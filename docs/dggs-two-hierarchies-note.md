# Your DGGS has two hierarchies

*(short note for discussion — Ben Griffin, 2026-07)*

Here's a five-line experiment. Take an H3 cell, ask for its res-5
ancestor two ways: by the index, and by asking which res-5 cell contains
its centre.

```python
import h3
leaf = '87390d509ffffff'                                  # res 7
idx  = h3.cell_to_parent(leaf, 5)                         # index ancestor
geo  = h3.latlng_to_cell(*h3.cell_to_latlng(leaf), 5)     # containing cell
print(idx, geo, idx == geo)
# 85390d53fffffff 85390d57fffffff False
```

Different cells. Neither answer is a bug. They are answers to two
different questions, and I think current DGGS practice — including the
draft OGC API — is ambiguous about which question its hierarchy
operations answer.

**The two relations.** Any non-congruent DGGS — any system whose cells
don't nest exactly across resolutions, which is every hexagonal or
pentagonal one (congruent quadtrees like S2 are the special case where
this all collapses) — supports:

1. a **lineage (index) relation** — identifier truncation. Transitive by
   construction: parent∘parent = grandparent. This is what
   `cellToParent` and friends actually compute.
2. a **geometric (containment) relation** — which coarser cell actually
   contains this cell (under some ownership rule for the straddlers).
   Coherent at every depth, but **not transitive**: the containing cell
   at K is not generally the parent-of-the-parent-of-…

And these cannot be reconciled. Non-nesting cells force a fringe
somewhere; the only choice is where you put it. Iterating the index
relation *compounds* it — descendants drift out of their ancestor. The
drift isn't a rare corner case. Classifying one cell's index-descendants
three levels down by overlap with the ancestor region: H3 has 12 of 343
entirely outside; A5 has 22 of 64; S2, 0 of 64 (nesting — the special
case). On our system (Hex9) we can dial the relation while holding the
grid fixed: the iterated-index reading puts exactly 1/6 of the
descendant area outside the ancestor — at every depth, it never
converges — while the geometric reading has zero fully-outside
descendants, exactly 9ᵈ of them, tiling the ancestor to within a
one-leaf fringe.

**The resolution that worked for me**: the hierarchy doesn't really live
on the public cells at all. It lives on whatever exact substructure the
refinement recurses on — the **carrier**. For Hex9 that's a rep-9
half-hexagon: the half-hex tree nests exactly, is transitive, and the
public hexagons are reified from it *once, at the leaf*. Ancestry
becomes exact integer arithmetic at any depth, and the non-transitivity
of the public-cell relation stops being a defect — it's the shadow of a
perfectly transitive tree one level down. I suspect the same move is
available to most systems: H3's carrier is the Gosper limit (no finite
polygon, but the geometric ancestor is still exactly computable in
lattice arithmetic — the snippet above is the float version of it); A5
presumably has an exact sub-pentagonal decomposition that could carry it.

**Why it matters at the API level.** OGC API–DGGS (21-038r1) built its
hierarchy on a *third* relation — the **sub-zone**, i.e. coverage:
everything at depth d that overlaps the parent. Right for retrieval and
paging; but it's not a partition, so zonal statistics over `parent-zone`
listings double-count every straddler — while a client composing native
`cellToParent` steps decoheres instead. The standard even admits the
symptom (Note 2 to its sub-zone definition: children-of-children are
not all sub-zones), and there's an open issue (#106) sharpening the
coverage definition. What's missing is the *ownership* reading: each
zone at depth d assigned to exactly one ancestor, exactly Nᵈ per
ancestor, partitioning the globe — the relation aggregation actually
needs, and (being non-transitive) the one that must be served at its
target depth rather than iterated by clients. The depth machinery
(`zone-level`) already exists; only the relation is missing.

I posted this as an issue on the OGC API–DGGS repo (building
on #106). 

Before I do: does this match your experience? Is the
